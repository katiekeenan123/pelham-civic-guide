How to Get Involved

Civic engagement doesn't require running for office. The most consistent voices in local government are often ordinary residents who show up. Here's where to start.

In Pelham, the people making these decisions live on your street. Most concerns are best raised the same way you'd raise anything with a neighbor — directly. These are ways to be heard, roughly in order of how many people they reach.
1

Start with a conversation

Your elected officials are your neighbors. A direct email or call is often the most effective and personal way to raise a concern — and the fastest. Most issues never need to go further than this.

2

Attend a public meeting

Speak during public comment to put your concern on the record. It becomes part of the official minutes, which means it is something the board has to reckon with rather than something it can simply note.

3

Write a letter to the editor

Amplify your voice and reach other residents. The Pelham Examiner publishes letters from residents, and officials read them.

4

Organize with neighbors

Collective voices carry more weight. Several residents raising the same specific concern — at a meeting, in letters, or in a shared email — is markedly harder to set aside than one.

🗳️

Vote in Every Election

Local races are decided by dozens — sometimes single-digit margins. Make sure you're registered and know what's on the ballot.

  • Register at elections.ny.gov or your county board of elections
  • Village/Town elections: November
  • School District budget vote: every May at PMS gym
  • County and State: November general elections
  • Board of Education seats: elected annually in May
🎤

Speak at a Public Meeting

Never spoken at a meeting before? You're not alone — and you don't need an appointment, a lawyer, or a perfect speech. A calm, specific comment from a resident carries real weight. Elected officials hear directly from people in the room in a way they don't from emails or social media.

What to Expect
  • Arrive 10–15 min early — ask the clerk how public comment works that evening; each board handles it slightly differently
  • When comment is called — stand up or raise your hand; walk to the microphone when recognized
  • State your name and street — "Good evening, my name is [NAME] and I live on [STREET]." That's it. You've started.
  • You get 2–3 minutes — a timer or light may signal your time; 150 words is plenty
  • The board will listen without responding — that's normal, not dismissive
  • Your comment becomes public record — meetings are recorded and often streamed
  • The nerves mean you care — residents who show up thoughtfully are remembered far longer than polished speeches
Tips for a Strong Comment
  • Say one thing specifically — "I'm concerned about the 40% spike in debt service and want a 10-year forecast presented publicly" lands harder than "taxes are too high in general"
  • Make a clear ask — "I'd like the board to..." gives officials something to respond to
  • Use your experience — you live here; personal impact is compelling evidence
  • Stay calm and constructive — boards hear a lot of frustration; a composed, specific ask stands out and is more likely to get a real response
  • Write it out first — even a few bullet points help you stay on track under pressure
  • Practice out loud twice — not to memorize, just so the words feel familiar in your mouth
Simple Comment Template
"Good evening. My name is [Name] and I live on [Street].

I'm here to speak about [topic].

[One or two sentences on how it affects you specifically.]

I'm asking the board to [specific request].

Thank you for your time."
You can also email your comment to your board before the meeting — it becomes part of the official record even if you can't attend.

Run for Office

Local offices — Village Trustee, Town Council, Board of Education — are genuinely accessible. Many current elected officials started exactly where you are: as concerned residents.

  • Village Trustee: 2-year term, part-time role
  • Town Councilor: 4-year term
  • Board of Education: 3-year term, elected in May
  • Contact the Westchester Board of Elections for petitioning requirements
  • Reach out to local party committees for guidance
