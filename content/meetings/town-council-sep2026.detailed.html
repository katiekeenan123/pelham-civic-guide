0:03–0:19Independent Audit — Fiscal Year Ended December 31, 2025

The town's outside auditor (not named audibly) presented results for the Town and its cemetery, using a slide deck plus a detail handout.

Opinion: unmodified — a clean opinion, described as "the highest level of assurance an entity can receive" — confirming the financial statements fairly present the town's position as of December 31, 2025.

Internal controls: some control deficiencies noted, but none rising to a material weakness or significant deficiency; several had already been corrected by management. No uncorrected differences — every proposed audit journal entry was recorded. The auditor flagged forthcoming GASB pronouncements and committed to working with the controller on them.

General fund: opening balance ~$2.2M, closing ~$2.45M, unassigned ~$2.1M after three years of growth — about 20% of the 2026 adopted budget, roughly 60% above the GFOA benchmark of two months' operating expenses. The original budget planned to draw $288,000 of fund balance for tax relief; a mid-year amendment added $346,000 in expenditures (including $176,000 for recreation and instructional items) and raised the planned draw to $626,000. The town instead ran a surplus. $350,000 of fund balance is being used to balance the 2026 budget as tax relief.

Revenue detail: interest and penalties ~$50,000 better than final budget; departmental income $2.8M against $2.4M budgeted (~$480,000 favorable, mostly parks and recreation charges, offset by higher spending in the same category); use of money and property ~$182,000 against $110,000 budgeted; sale of property and insurance recoveries $245,000 against $8,000 budgeted; mortgage tax $392,000 against $350,000. An unbudgeted state ambulance grant also landed. Revenues beat the final budget by $1.17M. Ambulance expenditures came in at ~$1.9M, $391,000 over budget, partly offset by savings elsewhere. The town exceeded the tax cap in 2025 after adopting the required local law.

Board response: members called it the most thorough presentation the town's accountant has given, and noted this was the first time the financials had been presented in public. The auditor advised continued monitoring of ambulance operations — separating recurring from non-recurring revenue and weighing pension and OPEB obligations — with ambulance insurance costs specifically flagged for 2027 budget development.

0:21–0:33EMS Report — Five Simultaneous Calls on September 6

The September 6 event: five EMS calls inside roughly three hours. Four were serious, including at least two cardiac arrests; the fifth was a lower-acuity fall. At peak, four paramedics and three ambulances were working in the Town of Pelham at once. Mutual-aid partner EVAC supplied two paramedics and two additional ambulances. The fire department — which does not normally run EMS first response — responded to cardiac arrest calls, and Pelham Police assisted on one. A board member contrasted this with three years ago, before the EVAC partnership, when waits could exceed 30 minutes.

Operations: the service is not billed for mutual-aid calls and will begin tracking person/lost-time data and participating more actively in the regional mutual-aid system from October. The Deputy Supervisor attended the first Quality Assurance meeting, which focused on response times, call types and mutual-aid frequency; a hospital is already represented and another may join.

Hospital turnaround was named an ongoing problem — sample times of 19, 25, 20, 44, 45, 45, 30 and 75 minutes, averaging about 30 at one frequently used facility, during which the ambulance is out of the service area. The service averages 2.6 calls a day. Contingency plans run from B through E when an ambulance is tied up.

Clarification for residents: some older residents believe the ambulance will not take them to their preferred hospital. Not so — the service transports to a patient's primary hospital where possible.

Countywide plan: New York State requires Westchester County to produce a comprehensive countywide EMS plan by the end of 2026. A regional meeting was set for the Wednesday after this one, with the Village of Pelham and Village of Pelham Manor represented. A local EMS Committee including residents and both village and town representatives is still being finalized.

0:35–0:46Library Report

Audit follow-up: the library was covered in the town audit process, which recommended a regular, formalized communication process between the library and the town on how expenses are recognized, categorized, allocated and invoiced.

Transformation project: a letter from the Library Board of Trustees, posted in late August, set out the board's current position. The library board is awaiting further information before deciding whether to proceed or pause.

Governance and budget clarity: the library is an independent corporate entity with its own audit; the town's audit references library funding but does not cover it. During budget season it emerged that some town spending benefiting the library — landscaping, for example — was never allocated to the library fund line. The town has asked that those costs be separated, and the library board agrees. Residents should expect year-over-year comparisons to need apples-to-apples adjustment as a result: the categorization changed, not the spending.

August numbers: 5,579 door visits; 271 children enrolled in summer reading, earning 97 cookie, 113 pizza-slice and 74 ice cream prizes; 242 children in in-house scavenger hunts; 63 adults in summer reading Bingo, the winner receiving a Kindle; 702 adults playing "My Turn at the Library"; 3,700 households on the newsletter.

Coming up: Kids Day at the Library, historically the library's biggest month for attendance and programming, aimed at kindergartners getting a first library card — each receives a bag with a book and a stuffed reading buddy, alongside crafts and a petting zoo. Booktemberfest, the Oktoberfest-themed fundraiser, with tickets via a banner on the library homepage — note this recording gives the date as October 5, where the August 3 meeting confirmed October 3, 5–8pm.

Library cards run three years and renew in person with proof of residency; cardholders are emailed on expiry. The three-year term is set by the shared library system, not by Pelham, and the town pays into that system based on active card count. New library board leadership takes effect October 1 (names not clear on the recording).

1:02–1:08Receiver of Taxes — Resignation, Vacancy and a Possible Referendum

The Supervisor opened the item by confirming the receiver resigned after accepting a job that did not allow her to hold public office, and said she had checked the position with the state association of towns' attorney. Under Town Law the Deputy Receiver assumes all responsibilities automatically — and continues to until someone is appointed or elected. A part-time employee covers when the deputy is out; a board member pressed on continuity of coverage and was told that gap is understood.

The referendum question: the Supervisor noted the deputy already performs nearly all the duties, while elected receivers have brought oversight rather than tax expertise, and asked whether the board should put a referendum to the town that would eliminate the elected office and make the position appointed by the board. Asked where oversight would then sit, she said with the supervisor and the controller, who already audit tax collections. The warrant process — warrants arriving from the school board, the villages and the county — was explained; collections are almost entirely electronic, with a very small share in cash.

Timing: the vacancy falls within three months of the election, so the seat cannot go on this November's ballot. An appointee would serve until the end of next year; an elected successor's term would begin January 2028; a referendum could go to voters next year, possibly alongside the election.

Next steps: the Supervisor proposed opening the appointment process to the public, as the board has done with other vacancies. A board member asked for research into towns that have no elected receiver and how they made the change, noting 19 area municipalities have an elected receiver and five do not. The deputy receiver was thanked for taking on more than the role normally requires. No motion was made and no vote taken — the full discussion was set for the October meeting.

1:08–1:19Board of Assessment Review — Paid for Years Without a Resolution

It emerged that appointed Board of Assessment Review members have been receiving per-session stipends without any formal resolution authorizing the compensation. The money was budgeted; it was simply never separately resolved.

The figures: roughly $30 per session for members and $40 for the chair — $20 was also mentioned, and the exact amount is to be confirmed. Sessions are not fixed in length; there are about three morning and three evening sessions a year, averaging around $200 per member per year across five members, against a budget line first stated as $1,300 and corrected to $1,600. The board noted other towns cap technical board compensation annually, while recognition and library boards generally go uncompensated.

Outcome: compensation stays at existing budget levels, not to exceed $1,600. Resolution language was started but not completed, and the item was deferred to October so the per-session figures can be confirmed and written in properly. No formal vote was recorded.
