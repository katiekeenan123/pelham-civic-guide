0:00–0:07President's Opening — Technology, Bond, Audit & Donations

Board President De Angelis previewed the evening's three presentations: technology in the schools, the bond projects, and the annual audit. On technology she gave her own view: "Particularly at the elementary level, I think we should prioritize human engagement and pen and paper whenever possible," while making sure students build the skills they need and technology supports individual learning needs. 0:07–0:14Superintendent's Report — PSAT, Playground Rules & Siwanoy Gates

Dr. Champ reported that 10th and 11th graders took the PSAT that day, ninth graders had a bonding day at Glover Field, and seniors attended college seminars. One student was named a National Merit semifinalist and 13 were commended; they will be honored at a November meeting with the NYSSMA All-State students.

Playgrounds: School grounds are closed to the public during school hours. Bikes, scooters and skateboards are allowed on outdoor play areas but not on the rubber surfaces near play equipment. E-bikes and electric scooters are not allowed on district playgrounds at all, a decision made with the fire chief because of lithium battery risks; students who ride them to school must lock them at a rack away from the building. Grounds close at dusk and open at dawn.

Siwanoy gates: Siwanoy has no swipe access system yet; one is planned as part of the bond work, so the district does not want to spend on it now. In the meantime the gates are locked at dusk on weekdays and left open all weekend. On a couple of recent weekends they were left locked by mistake and were reopened within about an hour.

0:20–0:52Technology Presentation — "Mindful Innovation"

Introduced by Dr. Comerford [VERIFY], Director of Technology John Sabales [VERIFY] and instructional specialist Alicia Del Mastro [VERIFY] described four years of moving away from the "crisis decisions" of the pandemic, when devices and apps were rolled out quickly. District leaders and the board read The Anxious Generation together last year. The two stated priorities are protecting young children's social, emotional and cognitive development by limiting screens, and preparing older students with gradually increasing responsibility.

"Screen value," not "screen time": The guiding principle is "purpose before minutes," favoring active uses such as coding, simulation and design over passive consumption. Teachers are trained on the SAMR model and the American Academy of Pediatrics' "Five Cs," and a new EdTech value matrix sorts tools into protect, allow, allow with guardrails, reduce or eliminate, and safeguard (for example, assistive technology in IEPs and 504 plans). Last year's software audit cut $100,000 of little-used pandemic-era tools.

District-wide practices: no devices at K–8 lunch or recess; no screen time as a reward unless an IEP calls for it; no unstructured "Fun Friday"-style use; administrators review ClassLink usage data by app, class and grade weekly. K–5 devices stay at school, grades 6–12 remain one-to-one, homework is not assigned on technology as standard practice, and K–2 logins for home platforms are no longer provided. The district has written grade-band guidance from kindergarten through high school.

Board questions: Asked whether the field is pushing toward more technology, the presenters said the district does not consider fixed daily limits best practice and knows of no scholarly or medical evidence for them. Some nearby districts have set time limits and others have opened up AI access; Pelham has done neither.

1:01–1:20AI Rules, Standards & Filtering

Teacher and K–12 student AI guidelines were released at the March 2026 Superintendent's Conference Day after about two years of work by an AI user group. K–5: no student-facing generative AI. Middle and high school: no open access; Gemini, ChatGPT and Claude are blocked, Classroom Gemini and Google's AI search are turned off for all students, and the only approved student tool is School AI [VERIFY], used only when a teacher directs it. The presenters called this "really restrictive" and the right approach for now, and plan to return later in the year with student and teacher feedback.

New York's 2024 Computer Science and Digital Fluency Standards are being taught "unplugged first" for the youngest students. Middle school technology teachers and librarians teach AI literacy with Common Sense Media's digital citizenship curriculum; high school teachers have recommended lessons. DreamBox, a non-generative math program, must be teacher-directed and kept to a small part of class time. Teachers using GoGuardian can see student screens in real time; the presenters acknowledged filtering is imperfect and students use workarounds such as VPNs.

A second parent asked for more forums such as town halls and workshops. The parent cited a Wall Street Journal article on Google and AI in schools, PISA results showing daily AI users scoring 20 points lower in science, and a Google research report warning of risks to children such as "diminished intellectual engagement" and an "illusion of mastery." The parent questioned a 17-minute average daily screen time figure attributed to the district, saying it leaves out smart boards and classroom video and hides the spread between students, and asked why de-identified data is not shared and why parents cannot see their own child's. The parent said no longer sending K–2 logins home reduces parents' view of classroom use, and asked for a K–2 Chromebook opt-out and paper-and-pencil alternatives for technology-based homework.

A board member thanked the speakers and said technology and screen time have been debated on the board for at least five years. Dr. Champ noted the October 13 parent session and said she would email Mr. Santo the next day.

1:36–2:10Annual Audit — Year Ended June 30, 2026

The partner from PKF O'Connor Davies, introduced by Jim, the Assistant Superintendent, reported an unmodified ("clean") opinion, the highest level of assurance, along with the government auditing standards report. There were no fraud, illegal acts, proposed adjustments or disagreements with management. Federal spending came in just under the new $1 million threshold, so no federal compliance audit was required.

Revenue and spending: Revenues came in about $926,000 above the roughly $91 million budgeted, helped by sales tax and state aid. Spending was about $86.7 million against a revised budget of about $90.4 million, roughly $3.5 million under. Special education, transportation and employee benefits all rose, with pension rates up after state Tier 6 changes.

Fund balance: The budget voters approved planned to use $2.5 million of fund balance, revised to $2.9 million to pay tax certiorari settlements. Fund balance actually fell $524,000, from about $18.3 million to $17.8 million. A planned $2.2 million transfer from the debt service fund was not made; Jim and the Superintendent chose to keep it there to offset debt service on the coming bonds. Unassigned fund balance is $3.9 million, 4% of the budget, the state maximum. The district holds an S&P AAA rating, with fund balance on the low end of AAA-rated districts.

Debt: Four bonds dating to 2016 are outstanding, with $58.3 million of principal; existing debt service drops off around 2031, when new bond payments are expected to take its place. The capital projects fund shows a temporary deficit because work was paid for before borrowing closed.

Asked whether the tax certiorari reserve, down from $5.4 million to about $2.9 million in two years, is still enough, Jim said the district reviews its exposure with its attorney each year, the largest settlement in his tenure was about $400,000, and no large settlements are expected.

Siwanoy: the permit application is with the State Education Department and described as "extremely close." Bidding is planned for this fall, aiming to finish before the holidays, with groundbreaking on the addition targeted for spring 2027. The project is on budget. The new bond steering committee will meet for the first time in October and then every four to five weeks.

2:29–3:02NYSSBA Resolutions — Direction to the Delegate

Trustee Cohen led a review of resolutions coming to NYSSBA's annual business meeting on October 15, which become part of the association's advocacy platform. The board gave her general discretion in case resolution text changes before the vote.

Resolution 20 (nonpartisan school board elections): oppose. Members supported nonpartisan elections in principle, but said the resolution would go beyond current state law, which already provides a nonpartisan ballot, by restricting party endorsements and campaign materials, raising First Amendment concerns. Trustee Cohen said it is widely unsupported across districts.

Resolutions 18 and 19 (dyslexia screening): oppose. The district does not do universal dyslexia screening; it uses universal reading screeners and refers students at risk for further assessment, and staff noted that dyslexia is a medical diagnosis. Members called Resolution 19 an unfunded mandate. Resolution 18 drew more discussion: members said its funding request appears only in its last sentence, and a board member said legislators should not be "mandating educators how to do their work," adding that if educators recommended screening every student, "I'm going to absolutely listen to that." The board will oppose 18 as written but could support it if amended to be clearly about funding. Members stressed that opposing these resolutions is not opposing support for students with dyslexia or dysgraphia.

Resolution 21 (school lunch flexibility): support, as a way to reduce waste. Lockdown drills: support keeping the current number, which a member called part of Pelham's "skillful and thoughtful" approach.
