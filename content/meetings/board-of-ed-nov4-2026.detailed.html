0:28–0:38Non-Resident Tuition Students (Item 6.2)

Dr. Champ gave the board its annual notice under the non-resident student policy. Non-resident students are admitted only if they fit existing classes, with no new sections or staff, and are in good standing. In total 77 tuition students attend:
• 48 staff children, at rates set in the collective bargaining agreements, which Dr. Champ called a great benefit in hiring talented staff.
• 24 students from "Diplomats Row", homes in the Bronx reached from a Pelham street, under an intermunicipal agreement with New York City dating to about the 1940s; New York City pays full tuition, just over $500,000 a year.
• Private pay: after several years without, the district budgeted to accept up to five private-pay students this year at the state-set tuition rate, at the high school only, where they are easiest to absorb. Three have been accepted so far, and one agreement is on tonight's agenda (Item 6.12).
• A few special education students placed by other districts, which pay the higher special education rate.

For homes split between Pelham and a neighboring municipality, state law lets the family choose the district, with reimbursement between districts. Voting in school elections follows residency, so Diplomats Row families cannot vote in Pelham.

0:53–1:15Public Comment — Screens in Kindergarten Through Second Grade

Three parents spoke, all on one-to-one devices in the early grades.

Gina Malik [VERIFY], parent of a first grader at Hutchinson and a preschooler, spoke for a new parent and community group, the Pelham Learning Alliance. Her three concerns: too much control of children's education given to tech companies; Chromebooks in K–2 with "no data showing that these tools are as good or better than traditional learning with a teacher"; and the need for more transparency and dialogue with parents in developing the district's tech policy. She said New York City, Los Angeles and Croton-Harmon have removed one-to-one devices in K–2, and Bronxville has removed devices in kindergarten and made two days a week tech-free. She cited research linking more screen time with poorer outcomes, said parents were asked at the start of the year to buy headphones without being told what they were for, and said children come home describing computer games and videos parents know nothing about. She described a Google announcement as meant "to intimidate and force school systems into using AI." She said some technology has a place in K–2, such as accessibility tools and a shared classroom screen in a teacher-led lesson. A board member thanked her, said the district is "in the midst of an initiative around this" with several parent meetings already held, and confirmed she was receiving the notices.

John Moynihan [VERIFY], a kindergarten parent who said the schools were the reason for moving to Pelham, was surprised to learn that a kindergartener was using one-to-one technology in class. "Our youngest Pelham children are now the guinea pigs of an unregulated ed tech industry," Mr. Moynihan said, asking the board to act as the regulator, and criticized gamified apps' rewards of "coins and pop-ups of confetti" as building extrinsic rather than intrinsic motivation. "The time for debate can be, should we implement it, not should we remove it," Mr. Moynihan said, asking the board to remove one-to-one technology in K–2 and not "come back month after month."

Tom Santer [VERIFY], a K–5 parent who also spoke in October, congratulated the students honored that night and presented research: an fMRI study from Children's National comparing reading a book with a narrated video; an Australian observational study of myelin in young children with high screen time; and a Spanish meta-analysis of 54 studies on the "screen inferiority effect" in reading. He said a middle school parent had reported having no physical textbooks for most classes. He cited the state's proposed FOCUS Act but said the district need not wait for legislation, asking at a minimum that parents be allowed to opt their children out of devices in the early grades and at most that the devices be removed.

The policies return for a second reading.

1:30–1:59Board & Administration Discussion — Screen Use

Where the district is: an administrator said the district has spent about 18 months in a research phase, is now setting guidelines and collecting baseline data from teachers on actual classroom use, and is "not waiting for the state to pass a law." The administrative team has begun monthly learning walks through classrooms; on the first, at the high school, Chromebooks were closed in every classroom visited. The guidelines given to teachers: in kindergarten, at most brief, teacher-guided technology supporting movement and hands-on play; in grades 1–2, selective digital tools for accessibility and language support, never replacing teaching of literacy and numeracy.

Next steps: Dr. Comerford [VERIFY] said the district agrees with the three main points raised, but that a business meeting "is not a discourse situation." Teachers are being told that if a screen is just replacing instruction, "don't do it." The district is gathering data from classrooms, research and all stakeholders, on a timeline of months, within this school year: feedback sessions with the PTAs, including SEPTA; site-based sessions with teachers; a "World Café" event for open discussion; and a survey of students, teachers and parents.

Board members' comments: members said they share the parents' concerns and that Pelham tries to be "responsive, not reactive." Several asked parents to say specifically what more "transparency" would mean to them, and suggested the district communicate what it is considering, what it decides and when, possibly with an end-of-year re-evaluation. Members encouraged parents to talk with teachers directly, from a place of curiosity, and noted that teachers adjust in real time. Members read the Google news differently: one understood that AI features are so embedded in search that turning them off means turning off search, and said they would re-read the article; another suggested Google could decouple them if it wanted to. A cabinet member said the whole cabinet, which meets weekly with Dr. Champ, attended a BOCES session with 15–20 other districts on the subject. Administrators said most of New York's roughly 730 districts have guidelines rather than formal policies on AI because policies are hard to reverse, and described the district's approach as "go slow to go fast," focused on whether each use is beneficial rather than on time limits.
