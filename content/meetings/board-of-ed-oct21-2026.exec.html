📅 October 21, 2026 ⏱ 102 min 🏫 Board of Education 👥 Board President De Angelis (chair) ▶ Watch recording

A Board of Education work session devoted to school finance and early planning for the 2027–28 budget. Superintendent Dr. Champ and the cabinet presented ten years of data on programs, staffing, student needs and costs; board members then met in two small groups and gave the administration their guidance for the budget. The only votes were procedural. No members of the public spoke.

The administration says the district is running lean. About 85% of the budget goes to salaries and benefits. Enrollment has held roughly steady over the decade. Staffing has grown, mainly in clinical support (psychologists and social workers), special education and intervention. Administration and clerical staff are each up only one position in ten years, and the district has one fewer custodian than a decade ago. In that time it has added buildings and fields, and it now hosts more than 6,600 after-school building-use events a year. After years of reductions through attrition, schedule changes, shared services and larger secondary classes (some now 25–28 students), Dr. Champ described the district as “very, very lean” and said further cuts could reach programs.

Special education needs are rising. The number of students with IEPs has grown 21% over ten years and 504 plans 148%, and the intensity of need is increasing even as the classification rate holds at about 13%, below the county’s 18%. Families are moving to Pelham for its programs, and a state change now lets students stay in school until age 22 with no added state funding. Administrators expect that cost to reach the secondary level in four to five years.

Universal Pre-K is coming, at an unknown cost. The state is requiring full-day (six-hour) pre-K for four-year-olds, and the district has no space for it in its buildings. This year it will issue RFPs for community-based partners to run it. A limited lottery pilot is planned for 2027–28, with expanded full-day pre-K for all interested families in 2028–29. The state pays $10,000 per student, which the administration expects will not cover the full cost. An RFP several years ago drew no responses.

Finances are sound but under pressure. Healthcare rose about 10% last year, and utilities, transportation and insurance are climbing; Jim, the Assistant Superintendent, said the Tier 6 pension retirement age is being lowered from 63 to 58, and a board member warned that rolling back Tier 6 adds strain on top of other state mandates. The district holds an S&P AAA rating, one of four districts in the state with it, though its fund balance is lower than the other three’s. Last year ended with a drawdown of about $500,000, of which about $425,000 covered a tax certiorari liability already set aside, leaving what Jim called “basically an even budget” on about $100 million. The athletics office, serving about 850 students on roughly 80 teams, still has one athletic director and one clerical assistant. Vice President Marrero called it “very understaffed” and said busing has been problematic since the pandemic.

The board’s guidance: present at least three budget versions (current services, current services with Universal Pre-K, and one beyond that); keep the AAA rating, a low fiscal stress score and a structurally sound budget; maintain current services; rethink responsibilities before adding staff; and show, with “tangible” evidence, which programs are stretched thin enough to be at risk. A board member also flagged the state’s New York Inspires overhaul as a coming cost. Budget calendar: the Superintendent’s budget launches February 22, line-by-line discussions begin March 8, and the budget vote is May 18. A ThoughtExchange community survey follows this session.
