0:00–0:08Opening & Purpose of the Work Session

Board President De Angelis opened the work session, focused on school finance and the considerations that will shape the 2027–28 budget, plus Universal Pre-K, which the state requires the district to begin offering on a limited basis next year, and the athletics program. Dr. Champ was appointed clerk pro tem.

Superintendent Dr. Champ said October is the board’s budget guidance month and the business office has begun assembling the budget. A summary of the session and a ThoughtExchange survey will go to the community afterwards. Dr. Champ noted the October 13 parent session on screen time, AI and instructional technology led by Mr. Sabalos [VERIFY] and Del Mastro [VERIFY], and a board coffee the next morning at 9 a.m. at Shae Station [VERIFY] to continue that conversation.

0:08–0:23Ten Years of Programs, Outcomes & Facilities

Dr. Champ, now in a tenth year in the district alongside Jim, the Assistant Superintendent, reviewed a decade of investment under three strategic plans. Highlights included professional learning and mentoring, MTSS, wider access to advanced courses, a vocational program grown from about 10 to about 30 students, and integrated co-teaching (ICT), now in place K–4 and two years from a full K–12 continuum.

Outcomes cited: about 80% of students in grades 3–8 proficient or above on state ELA and math tests; the largest number of National Merit commended students in school history; strong results in science research, music and debate. On the district’s climate survey, more than 75% of middle school students and more than 90% of high school students say they feel safe at school. A fall climate survey launches within weeks.

Facilities: the 2018 bond built the new Hutchinson school and turfed most fields; the 2025 bond is upgrading infrastructure in buildings about 100 years old. The district bought Pelhamdale House for the business office and is renovating Franklin Place for central offices, so that all offices leave rented space by next school year. That growth in square footage means more maintenance and more events.

0:23–0:41Building Use, Staffing & Class Sizes

The district logged 6,631 after-hours building-use events in 2024–25, not counting outdoor fields. Community groups reimburse direct custodial costs under the annual facilities agreement; office and administrative costs are not billed.

About 85% of the budget is salaries and benefits. Enrollment has varied by only about 30 students across 13 grades, but staffing has grown for clinical support (a psychologist in each elementary school and social workers at the middle and high schools), special education, intervention and world languages. Administration and clerical staff are each up just one position in ten years, and the district has one fewer custodian than a decade ago despite more buildings, fully turfed fields and more events.

Trustee Cohen said the board should look at staffing “with a really critical eye” and ask whether it can support new programs, keep existing ones robust and maintain safe environments. Dr. Champ compared unsupported growth to an “upside down pyramid” that works “until it doesn’t.” Kindergarten and first grade are kept at 21 students (down from as high as 23), but some secondary classes have risen to 25–28. A student representative said upper-level classes are smaller, the largest about 20.

0:41–0:51Student Needs & Special Education

Over ten years the number of students with IEPs rose 21% and 504 plans 148%; about 15% of students qualify for free or reduced-price lunch. Tracey [VERIFY], who oversees pupil personnel services, said Pelham’s classification rate is about 13%, against 18% in Westchester and 20% statewide, but that the intensity of need is rising. Preschool special education enrollment hit a ten-year high of 60 in 2023–24, prompting an additional 8:1:2 class. The 12:1:2 classes are full, and nearly every student in special classes now uses a communication (AAC) device, up from one or two per class before the pandemic.

Families are moving to Pelham for its programs: in one recent group of incoming students with IEPs, about seven needed a program placement. A state change now lets students remain in school until age 22, with no added state funding, and administrators expect that cost to reach the secondary level in four to five years.

0:51–0:56Other Growing Workloads

Technology and cybersecurity have grown, with BOCES moving to a managed off-site model. Electronic registration now handles three grade levels at once, and residency checks have required added legal and investigative services. FOIL requests, some submitted by bots, now take up about half of one staff member’s job, and a single email request can mean days of work; administrators said this belongs on the district’s advocacy list. Mail-in ballots have added work for the district clerk. After-school care, run by the Pelham Children’s Center, has expanded to all four elementary schools. Universal free lunch is popular, but it has ended the food-service margins that once helped fund equipment and staff.

0:56–1:06Universal Pre-K

Dr. Champ called Universal Pre-K a state mandate the district would have offered sooner if it had the space. Dr. Comerford [VERIFY] described a phased plan for four-year-olds (not 3-K), six hours a day on the school calendar:
• This year (2026–27): RFPs, issued “soon” with the business office, to find community-based partners to house and run the program.
• 2027–28: a limited number of seats by lottery with the chosen partner.
• 2028–29: expanded full-day pre-K, by lottery, for all interested families.

The state now pays $10,000 per enrolled full-day student. The district plans to keep part of that for administration, but expects the real cost to exceed $10,000 a student, describing it as adding a grade level. In answer to Trustee Garcia, administrators said the district does not have to offer seats next year but wants to phase in, and that admission will be a random lottery with a wait list, not income-based. Jim said providers may offer paid wrap-around care before and after the six funded hours, but cannot require it. Jim added that an RFP three or four years ago drew no responses, and that with every district facing the mandate at once, seats will be scarce; next year’s pilot is meant to establish the full cost.

1:06–1:14Athletics

About 850 students, more than half of secondary students, play on roughly 80 teams; participation is up 19% in ten years and new sports have been added. The athletics office is still one athletic director and one clerical assistant, who process about 100 coaches’ certifications each year. A part-time scheduler and an equipment manager have been added. The interim athletic director is studying the program and will recommend how it can grow sustainably.

Vice President Marrero said the athletics office is “very understaffed” for the demands of students, parents, buses and fields, and that, “as it pains us to spend more,” those needs must be met. Marrero praised the staff’s nights, weekends and holidays and said busing has been “problematic” since the pandemic.

1:14–1:27Financial Pressures & Fund Balance

Jim, the Assistant Superintendent, said costs have outpaced growth in the tax levy: salaries, healthcare (up about 10% last year, mostly from pharmaceuticals), utilities, transportation, insurance and special education. Electricity supply comes through the New York Power Authority, but Con Edison delivery costs keep rising; the transportation contract includes fuel, which protects the district from diesel price swings. About half of employees are in the Tier 6 pension, whose retirement age is being lowered from 63 to 58. A board member warned that rolling back Tier 6 adds strain on top of other mandates.

Dr. Champ listed the tools used over ten years: attrition, redistributing work, restructuring schedules, shared services, larger classes within guidelines, advocating for state aid, and grants. Dr. Champ said the district is now “very, very lean” and further cuts could reach programs.

On fund balance, Jim said the board has used post-COVID savings for one-time costs such as rooftop HVAC units, playgrounds and the Franklin Avenue house. The district holds an S&P AAA rating, one of four districts in the state with it, but its fund balance is lower, as a share of budget and in dollars, than the other three. The auditors reported a drawdown of about $500,000 last year, about $425,000 of it for a tax certiorari liability already set aside, leaving “basically an even budget” on close to $100 million. Items for the coming budget: restoring some of last year’s temporary cuts, sustaining existing programs, and resourcing the strategic plan.

1:27–1:41Small Groups & Board Guidance

After off-camera table discussions, Board President De Angelis reported that the first table valued academic excellence, social-emotional learning, world-class facilities and student voice at every level, and wanted tangible outcomes for added resources and support for staff serving higher-need students. The second table listed access for all, high achievement, well-rounded development, robust offerings, keeping what the district has, prioritizing the classroom, high-quality staff and the strategic plan.

Guidance to the administration:
• Present at least three budget versions: current services, current services with Universal Pre-K, and one beyond that.
• Maintain the strong financial position: the AAA rating, a low fiscal stress score, prudent use of fund balance and a structurally sound budget.
• Maintain current services with no reductions, while feeling free to shift resources; apply systems thinking to new investments; rethink responsibilities before adding staff.
• Treat Universal Pre-K as a state-set priority.

Building on Trustee Cohen’s pyramid point, a board member asked the administration to show which programs are stretched thin enough to be at risk, since the board cannot see that itself. Members kept returning to “tangible” evidence to explain any increase to the community. A board member also flagged the state’s New York Inspires / Portrait of a Graduate overhaul as a cost; Claire [VERIFY] said budget needs center on professional learning, curriculum writing and assessment design over a five-year phase-in, with state clarity not expected before February.
